1. Executive summary and key price drivers 
Confirmed Total Hormuz Clearance was 9,867 kbd on 17 October, within a 4 -day range of 9,867 kbd to 13,067 kbd. 
The 7-day average of 10,173 kbd sits about 41% below the pre-war baseline of 17,267 kbd. Adding Gulf of Oman 
terminals and the Red Sea lifts the regional 7-day average to 19,041 kbd, about 19% below its baseline. Kpler Insight 
reports that at least 18 vessels were reportedly attacked over 2 weeks, which keeps the risk premium intact. A Houthi 
strike hit the Aramco headquarters in Dhahran on 13 October, causing a fire but leaving oil operations unaffected. 
Meanwhile, Asian crude imports reached 26 mbd in the first half of October, within 1% of the pre -war level, as earlier 
Gulf cargoes arrive.

Table and chart methodology and caveats 
● Every figure is a confirmed floor and can revise upward at any horizon. The recency tiers mark where 
revisions concentrate, not where they stop. 
● Reported figures are older than 14 days, provisional figures are 8 to 14 days old, and preliminary figures 
cover the last 7 days. Preliminary figures have typically settled 1.5 to 2 times higher once confirmations land.  
● Gulf of Oman, Kutch Light and Red Sea volumes with no confirmed load terminal capture ship -to-ship cargo 
net of unmatched arrivals, and they carry no origin attribution. The Iranian-origin exclusion therefore applies to 
strait transits only. 
● Pre-war baselines are averages over March 2025 to February 2026 on the same definitions and product -
bucket selection. 
● Strait crossings are dated by transit, unattributed ship-to-ship cargo by the date it surfaces or departs, and 
berth loadings by loading date. Daily totals therefore mix event timings, and the 7-day averages absorb most 
of that noise. 
Primary drivers 
● Attacks on commercial vessels keep clearance well below pre-war levels, and sections 2 and 3 set out the 
transit and fleet picture. 
● Bypass routes offset part of the loss, with Fujairah and Oman terminal loadings and Red Sea volumes above 
their pre-war baselines (section 2). 
● Saudi Arabia exports from both coasts, while Houthi strikes on Saudi sites keep the export recovery exposed 
(sections 3 and 4). 
● Record freight lifts delivered crude costs, and Kpler expects sanctions relief for shadow fleet tankers to do 
little to ease it (sections 3 and 5). 
● Diesel stays tight, and trend-following funds rebuilt maximum long exposure in ULSD (section 5). 
2. Chokepoint and transit metrics 
A. Strait of Hormuz 
Confirmed strait transits, excluding Iranian-origin vessels, were 7,363 kbd on 17 October. The 7-day average was 
7,894 kbd, against a pre-war baseline of 17,267 kbd. Daily readings are preliminary floors and typically revise upward 
as confirmations land. Kpler Insight says the current decline in transits fits its view that the recovery in the strait will be 
uneven. 
Gulf of Oman ship-to-ship net exports were 2,504 kbd on 17 October, after 328 kbd on 16 October. Their 7 -day 
average of 2,280 kbd compares with a pre-war level of 0 kbd. Fujairah and Oman terminal loadings were 766 kbd on 
17 October, after 9,428 kbd the day before. Their 7-day average of 4,563 kbd is about 34% above the pre-war 
baseline of 3,416 kbd. Bypass routes therefore continue to offset part of the loss at the strait.  
Kpler Insight reports that Chinese buyers have secured at least 80 mb of Saudi spot crude since mid -September, 
while domestic stocks fall at a record pace. 
B. Bab el-Mandeb and Red Sea 
Red Sea volumes were 11,585 kbd on 17 October, after 3,593 kbd on 16 October and 6,919 kbd on 15 October. The 
7-day average of 4,304 kbd compares with a pre-war baseline of 2,803 kbd, about 54% above. Red Sea volumes 
include cargo with no confirmed load terminal, so the latest readings are preliminary. Kpler Insight reports that Sidi 
Kerir, the Mediterranean end of Sumed, is loading around 1 mbd, mostly bound for South Korea and Japan.

3. Maritime risk and fleet status 
Kpler Insight reports at least 18 vessels reportedly attacked over 2 weeks, with strait tanker crossings averaging 13 a 
day against 50 before the war. The running week also brought the first full sinking of a vessel in about 7 months, the 
containership GFS PRIDE, according to IMO data cited by Kpler Insight. A Houthi strike on the Aramco administrative 
building in Dhahran late on Tuesday 13 October caused a fire. A source at the Saudi energy ministry confirmed there 
were no injuries or fatalities. Kpler reported no direct implications for oil markets, because the strike did not affect 
anything beyond the headquarters building. 
Freight is the other pressure point. Kpler Insight reports that Middle East Gulf to China VLCC rates fell $1.09/bbl week 
on week to $37.37/bbl, easing from a record opening level of $38.47/bbl. More than 80 VLCCs work shuttle trades, 
and only around 42% of VLCCs are laden, against a 45% historical average. Kpler expects reported US sanctions 
relief for shadow fleet tankers to have no material impact on VLCC freight rates.  
Strait tanker crossings were 6 on 17 October, against a 7-day average of 10 and a pre-war level of 50. The latest day 
shows 2 entries and 4 exits. Kpler classed 34 of the 43 oil tanker crossings from 14 to 17 October as dark or unknown 
route transits. The table shows 8 of them.

4. Critical infrastructure status 
Saudi infrastructure, Petroline and Yanbu 
An attack on 10 September damaged a pumping station on the East-West Pipeline, also called Petroline. Kpler first 
assessed repairs at 4 to 6 weeks, but loadings at Yanbu and Muajjiz restarted on 17 September. Kpler analysis of 
satellite imagery confirmed that west coast crude loadings reached 42 mb between 17 September and 4 October. The 
pipeline was running at around 5.8 mbd on 12 October, according to industry sources.  
The Dhahran strike underlines the exposure of Saudi infrastructure, as the Houthis continue to target sites across the 
kingdom. The Saudi energy ministry plans to lift pipeline capacity to 10 mbd from 7 mbd, which could take up to 3 
years.

5. Refined products and bunker fuel impact 
Diesel stays the tightest part of the barrel. Gasoil rose 9.5% in the week to 9 October against 2.4% for Brent, after EU 
states declined to add to March stock releases. Kpler Insight attributes the move to policy rather than new demand 
and estimates the global diesel deficit at around 1 mbd as peak seasonal demand begins. A US authorisation of 
Russian diesel imports came too late to change the weekly print, and nearly 40% of Russian refining capacity is idle.  
Trend-following CTAs bought about 2.8 mb of ULSD in the reporting week from 6 to 13 October, returning to 100% 
long for the first time since 24 September. ULSD rose 8% week on week, with US Gulf Coast diesel cracks up 
$9.78/bbl. Gasoline fundamentals softened, and trend-following CTAs cut RBOB exposure from 91% to 82% long. 
Fuel oil also stays tight. Singapore fuel oil rose 7.4% as scarce cutter stocks kept high sulphur fuel oil in 
backwardation. Kpler Insight expects Singapore HSFO to stay supported until Russian and Middle Eastern arrivals in 
late October test the backwardation. 
Clean freight added to the squeeze, as LR2 tankers switched to more lucrative dirty cargoes. LR2 rates from the 
Middle East Gulf to Japan rose $1.5/bbl month on month to $24.96/bbl, and Kpler Insight sees further upside for clean 
freight from the region.

Standing methodology note, vessel and cargo tracking 
Kpler compiles vessel and cargo tracking data from 3 complementary sources. Automatic Identification System (AIS) 
signals form the backbone, providing real-time position data whenever a vessel's transponder is active. A gap in that 
signal is informative but not conclusive on its own. Satellite imagery, both optical and synthetic aperture radar (SAR), 
corroborates vessel presence and laden state at terminals and along transit routes. SAR can see through cloud cover 
where optical imagery cannot. These streams are supplemented by human intelligence, meaning market sources 
reporting loading events, ship-to-ship transfer scheduling and port call activity. This intelligence often flags a 
movement before satellite imagery can confirm it. 
The 3 streams are cross-referenced against a binary confirmation standard. An event is recorded only when 
consistent evidence from more than one source supports it, not through probabilistic attribution or a single source. 
Where AIS coverage lapses, such as during a dark transit, analysts look for satellite imagery placing a vessel inside a 
region on one date and outside it on another. They also draw on human intelligence and discharge -side confirmation 
that corroborates timing and cargo grade. Volumes that cannot be independently verified this way are classified as 
unknown origin rather than attributed by inference, a deliberately conservative approach that favours accuracy over 
completeness.
